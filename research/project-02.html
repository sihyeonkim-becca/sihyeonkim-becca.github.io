Budget-Constrained Green-Roof Spatial Optimization Using SOLWEIG and Genetic Algorithms

An AI-aided spatial optimization framework that identifies which individual rooftops should receive green-roof investment to maximize pedestrian cooling under fixed public budgets.

Background

The first Urban Heat Island study showed that green roofs produced the most consistent pedestrian-level cooling among the tested strategies. However, applying green roofs to every available building is financially unrealistic.

Traditional implementation plans often prioritize total roof area, building eligibility, or installation convenience. These approaches do not directly measure whether a particular green roof meaningfully improves the pedestrian environment. This project therefore asks a more specific planning question: which individual rooftops should be selected when the available public budget is limited? The research treats green-roof implementation as a spatial optimization problem rather than a simple area-maximization problem.

Research Questions

  • Which combination of rooftops maximizes pedestrian-level cooling under a fixed budget?
  • How does the value of additional investment differ across urban regeneration contexts?
  • When should planners stop investing in rooftops and redirect the remaining budget to ground-level strategies?

Optimization Framework

Each feasible rooftop was represented as a binary decision variable — 1 if selected for green-roof installation, 0 if not. A unit-level SOLWEIG-GPU simulation was conducted for each candidate rooftop to estimate its contribution to outdoor UTCI reduction. The resulting effects were stored in a precomputed lookup table, allowing a genetic algorithm to evaluate many rooftop combinations without rerunning the full physical simulation during every iteration.

The optimization was performed under six policy budget levels — KRW 0.2, 0.5, 0.7, 1.2, 2.0, and 3.0 billion. The primary objective was to maximize pedestrian cooling while satisfying the budget constraint. The analysis also evaluated meaningful cooling area, selected-building counts, marginal returns, and budget saturation.

Research Workflow

1. Candidate Building Preparation
Prepared building and land-cover data at 2 m resolution; identified structurally and spatially feasible rooftops; excluded unsuitable building types and areas; calculated implementation costs by rooftop area.

2. Unit-Level Thermal Simulation
Applied green roofs individually to candidate buildings; conducted SOLWEIG-GPU simulations; calculated rooftop-specific ΔUTCI raster outputs; identified meaningful pedestrian cooling using a minimum threshold.

3. Optimization
Encoded rooftop selection as binary variables; built a precomputed thermal-effect lookup table; implemented a genetic algorithm in Python; applied hard budget constraints; optimized rooftop combinations under six budget tiers.

4. Policy Interpretation
Compared total cooling effect; calculated meaningful cooling area; assessed marginal gains between budget levels; identified saturation and low-sensitivity conditions; proposed alternative ground-level interventions where roof investment was ineffective.

Abstract

Green roofs are increasingly promoted as an Urban Heat Island mitigation strategy, yet their pedestrian-level benefits vary substantially by building location and surrounding urban context. Because public budgets cannot support implementation on every feasible rooftop, this study develops a budget-constrained spatial optimization framework that integrates SOLWEIG-GPU microclimate simulation with a binary genetic algorithm.

Candidate rooftops were identified across three Seoul urban regeneration areas. Individual rooftop simulations were conducted to estimate their contribution to pedestrian-level UTCI reduction, and the results were stored in a precomputed lookup table. The genetic algorithm then selected rooftop combinations that maximized cooling under six fixed budget levels.

The results reveal three distinct investment patterns. Sinchon demonstrated scalable cooling benefits as the budget increased, indicating a broad pool of effective rooftop candidates. Changsin–Soongin produced smaller, spatially concentrated benefits and reached saturation before the maximum budget was exhausted. Shinwol-dong showed almost no meaningful pedestrian cooling despite a substantial number of feasible rooftops, indicating that rooftop investment alone was unsuitable in this context.

Methodological Contribution

The project connects three previously separated components: physical microclimate simulation, AI-based spatial search, and public budget constraints. The genetic algorithm does not simply generate a formally optimal design — it provides planning-relevant outputs such as priority rooftop maps, budget-performance curves, selected-building counts, meaningful cooling-area estimates, marginal return analysis, budget saturation points, and areas where rooftop intervention should be rejected.

Green-roof planning is not a problem of maximizing installed area; it is a problem of allocating limited investment to locations where rooftop cooling meaningfully reaches pedestrians.
