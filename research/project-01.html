Cost-Effective Urban Heat Mitigation across Seoul Urban Regeneration Typologies

An ENVI-met study evaluating how green roofs, bright roofs, and permeable pavement influence pedestrian thermal comfort and life-cycle cost across different urban regeneration contexts in Seoul.

Background

Urban Heat Island mitigation policies frequently recommend strategies such as green roofs, reflective roofs, and permeable pavement. However, these interventions are often applied as universal solutions without sufficient consideration of neighborhood-specific physical conditions.

In existing urban regeneration areas, planners cannot redesign the entire urban structure. Buildings, streets, parcels, topography, and infrastructure are already established. Therefore, mitigation must be implemented through selective retrofit strategies. The main challenge is that the same intervention may produce different pedestrian-level thermal effects depending on the surrounding urban context.

This research approaches Urban Heat Island mitigation as both an environmental performance problem and a public investment problem.

Research Questions

  • How do green roofs, bright roofs, and permeable pavement influence pedestrian thermal comfort across different urban regeneration typologies?
  • Which intervention provides the greatest thermal benefit relative to its construction and maintenance cost?

Study Design

The research began by reviewing Seoul's statutory urban regeneration plans and compiling physical, environmental, and socioeconomic data for officially designated regeneration areas.

Principal component analysis was used to reduce correlated variables, and K-means clustering was applied to classify the regeneration areas into representative typologies. Selected sites were then modeled using ENVI-met.

Three mitigation strategies were tested: green roofs, bright or cool roofs, and permeable concrete block pavement.

The environmental effect was evaluated using the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) rather than air temperature alone, since it represents the combined influence of air temperature, humidity, wind speed, and mean radiant temperature on human thermal stress.

The simulation outputs were then combined with installation and annual maintenance costs to assess ten-year economic performance — connecting spatial classification, microclimate simulation, pedestrian thermal comfort, and cost-effectiveness within a single planning framework.

Research Workflow

1. Urban Regeneration Typology Classification
Reviewed statutory regeneration plans; collected urban form, land cover, topographic, and socioeconomic indicators; cleaned spatial and tabular data using Python and GIS; applied PCA and K-means clustering; selected representative sites from different regeneration contexts.

2. Microclimate Modeling
Constructed three-dimensional ENVI-met models; defined building, vegetation, surface, and material conditions; simulated baseline and mitigation scenarios; validated air temperature and humidity against urban sensor observations.

3. Thermal Comfort Assessment
Calculated pedestrian-level UTCI; compared daytime, evening, and extended daily periods; examined spatial cooling and warming patterns; evaluated whether surface-level cooling translated into actual pedestrian benefit.

4. Economic Evaluation
Calculated applicable intervention areas; estimated installation and maintenance costs; compared thermal performance with life-cycle expenditure; derived context-specific investment recommendations.

Abstract

The urban heat island (UHI) effects is a major challenge that elevates summer air temperatures and increases energy demand in cities. Although various heat mitigation strategies have been implemented, many require substantial investment. In already developed urban areas, regeneration projects provide opportunities to introduce cost-effective alternatives that enhance outdoor thermal comfort. While numerous studies have addressed UHI mitigation, few have comprehensively analyzed the specific impacts of urban regeneration strategies. This study aims to fill this research gap by comprehensively evaluating the thermal performance and cost-effectiveness of representative mitigation strategies across urban regeneration areas in Seoul, considering their urban typologies.

Urban regeneration typologies were identified using k-means, an unsupervised learning method, from 53 officially designated urban regeneration projects in Seoul, and representative sites were selected from each typology cluster. Multiple mitigation strategies, including green roofs, bright roofs, and green pavement, and their combinations were applied using ENVI-met v5.8.0 simulations. The Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) was used to assess outdoor thermal comfort outcome of mitigations strategies under typical summer heat conditions in 2024 and validated using the S-DoT dataset. Cost-effectiveness was evaluated by comparing the total 10-year implementation cost of each strategy against its corresponding outdoor thermal comfort performance.

The results reveal strong typology dependent variability in both outdoor thermal comfort performance and economic efficiency. No single mitigation strategy consistently reduced thermal stress across all neighborhood typologies. Green roofs showed the highest absolute cooling potential, achieving up to a 0.42°C reduction in UTCI during peak hours between 13:00 and 15:00, particularly in dense urban conditions. Bright roofs showed context-dependent performance, providing moderate cooling in some cases while producing warming effects in others. Green pavement exhibited highly inconsistent thermal performance, often leading to daytime warming and only limited or delayed cooling effects. Cost-effectiveness results reveal a systematic decoupling between outdoor thermal comfort performance and economic efficiency, indicating that stronger cooling outcomes do not necessarily correspond to more cost-effective interventions.

These findings demonstrate that heat mitigation strategies can either alleviate or exacerbate thermal stress depending on urban typology. Green roofs provide the most consistent cooling, while green pavement poses the highest risk of adverse effects. Importantly, the results confirm that cooling effectiveness and economic efficiency are fundamentally conditioned by urban neighborhood typology rather than being intrinsic properties of individual strategies. This study highlights the critical need to integrate cost-effect consideration into heat mitigation decision-making, a dimension often overlooked in existing studies. By demonstrating that thermal and economic outcomes are fundamentally context dependent, this study provides an evidence-based framework to support more climate responsive and economically informed approaches to sustainable urban regeneration.

Main Findings

Green Roof

Green roofs provided the most stable cooling performance across the study sites. Their pedestrian-level effect was relatively modest in magnitude but consistent compared with the other strategies. This suggests that green roofs are suitable as a broadly reliable intervention, although their high construction and maintenance costs require strategic rather than indiscriminate implementation.

Bright Roof

Bright roofs produced weak and context-dependent pedestrian effects. In some areas, they generated slight cooling, while in others reflected shortwave radiation reduced or reversed the expected benefit at street level — demonstrating that lowering roof-surface temperature does not automatically improve pedestrian thermal comfort.

Permeable Pavement

Permeable pavement increased daytime pedestrian heat stress under dry simulation conditions in the study sites. The material may provide stormwater and hydrological benefits, but its thermal performance depends on moisture availability, evaporation, solar exposure, and heat storage. It should therefore not be automatically classified as an Urban Heat Island mitigation strategy.

Contribution

  • Evaluates Urban Heat Island interventions using pedestrian-level thermal comfort, rather than relying only on surface or air temperature.
  • Compares intervention performance across urban regeneration typologies, showing that the same strategy cannot be assumed to work equally in different neighborhoods.
  • Integrates microclimate effects with life-cycle costs, reframing urban heat mitigation as a resource-allocation decision.
Urban Heat Island mitigation should be selected according to neighborhood-specific thermal performance and economic feasibility, rather than universal assumptions about individual materials.
