Project 03 · SimplexNMF · Urban Morphology

Mixture of Urban Form Archetypes in Seoul: A Signal Processing Approach

A qualitative–quantitative urban morphology study combining SimplexNMF, hybrid clustering, historical literature, GIS overlays, and Sankey analysis to interpret Seoul as mixtures of evolving urban form archetypes.

Background

Seoul's contemporary urban fabric was formed through successive historical periods, including the late Joseon period, Japanese colonial planning, postwar reconstruction, rapid industrialization, apartment development, redevelopment, and urban regeneration.

Conventional clustering methods tend to classify urban areas into discrete types. However, Seoul's neighborhoods often contain overlapping and historically layered patterns rather than a single, pure urban form. This study conceptualizes contemporary urban morphology as a mixture of latent archetypes.

Research Approach

The quantitative component applied a newly developed SimplexNMF method to urban form indicators derived from 1,418 Seoul grid cells. The method identified nine latent archetypes and estimated their proportional mixture within each cell.

My contribution focused on linking these data-driven results with historical and morphological knowledge. I reviewed approximately 60 studies covering Seoul's urban development and synthesized urban form typologies across major historical periods, then connected the literature-based typologies with the quantitative mixture clusters through GIS overlays and a Sankey diagram.

The Sankey diagram illustrated how data-driven clusters corresponded to traditional forms such as organic development, urban hanok, redevelopment blocks, apartment complexes, hybrid superblocks, and neighborhood-unit superblocks.

Abstract

Seoul's urban morphology reflects multiple historical and planning regimes, yet conventional typological studies often represent the city as a collection of static and mutually exclusive classes. This study reframes urban form as mixtures of latent archetypes and applies a simplex-constrained nonnegative matrix factorization method to GIS-derived indicators for 1,418 neighborhood-scale grid cells.

Nine interpretable archetypes were identified, including fine-grained organic development, mixed low-rise areas, high-rise apartment districts, institutional complexes, waterfront patterns, hilly settlements, and park-edge fabrics. Hybrid clustering of the archetype weights revealed both dominant and mixed morphological patterns across Seoul.

To establish historical and planning meaning, the data-driven archetypes were compared with typologies synthesized from approximately 60 studies of Seoul's urban development history. GIS overlays and a Sankey diagram were used to connect mixture clusters with expert-defined typologies. Case studies of Yeongdong and Jamsil demonstrated how imported planning concepts, topography, land readjustment, market adaptation, and redevelopment generated hybrid urban forms.

The study shows that quantitative decomposition and qualitative urban history provide complementary interpretations, offering a scalable and historically grounded method for diagnosing neighborhood character and supporting context-sensitive urban regeneration.
