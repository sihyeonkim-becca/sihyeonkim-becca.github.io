About — Environmental Design to Urban Climate

From Building Performance to the Thermal City

My interest in cities began with architecture, where I learned to view the building as the smallest environmental unit surrounding human life. I was drawn less to architecture as visual form than to its capacity to improve comfort, health, and everyday experience.

Environmental design made that interest measurable. Through building-performance simulation, I tested how façade perforations, louvers, and window dimensions affected daylight, natural ventilation, glare, and energy demand. Adaptive-reuse and urban-design projects then expanded the same question beyond the building: how do infrastructure, public space, mobility, and surrounding urban conditions shape human well-being?

I came to understand that a building's environmental performance is inseparable from its context. Neighboring structures, street geometry, surface materials, vegetation, solar radiation, and airflow interact at the scale of blocks and neighborhoods. Urban planning therefore became, for me, an expansion of environmental design—from optimizing one building to improving the collective environmental conditions of the city.

At Seoul National University, this trajectory developed into Urban Heat Island research. I now integrate microclimate simulation, geospatial analysis, cost assessment, and AI-aided optimization to evaluate where heat-mitigation strategies work, how their performance changes across urban contexts, and how limited public resources can produce the greatest improvement in pedestrian thermal comfort.

Environmental design and urban climate planning are part of the same inquiry: how can spatial decisions create healthier conditions for human life?
